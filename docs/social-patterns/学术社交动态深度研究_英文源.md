## 1. Politeness Theory & Face-Threatening Acts (Brown & Levinson Framework)

### 1.1 Core Concepts

Brown and Levinson's politeness theory, built on Goffman's concept of "face," provides
the foundational framework for understanding academic interaction:

- **Positive face**: The desire to be appreciated, approved of, and connected to others
- **Negative face**: The desire for autonomy, freedom of action, and freedom from imposition

A **face-threatening act (FTA)** is any act that inherently damages the addressee's or
speaker's face by acting in opposition to their wants. FTAs can be:
- Verbal (words/language)
- Paraverbal (tone, inflection, pace)
- Non-verbal (facial expression, posture, eye contact)

### 1.2 Four Politeness Strategies (Ranked by Indirectness)

| Strategy | Description | Academic Example |
|----------|-------------|------------------|
| **Bald on-record** | Direct, unmitigated FTA | "This methodology is wrong." |
| **Positive politeness** | FTA + appeal to solidarity | "Great effort, but I think we need to revisit the method." |
| **Negative politeness** | FTA + hedging/deference | "I wonder if there might be some issues with the methodology?" |
| **Off-record (indirect)** | Hints, implicature | "Interesting approach... I've seen some papers use a different method for this kind of data." |

### 1.3 Three Social Variables Governing Strategy Choice

1. **Power (P)**: Relative power between speaker and hearer (professor > student)
2. **Social Distance (D)**: Degree of familiarity (same lab vs. external reviewer)
3. **Ranking of Imposition (R)**: How face-threatening the specific act is (suggesting minor edit vs. questioning entire premise)

**Actionable Pattern**: The greater the combined weight of P + D + R, the more indirect
the strategy chosen. A senior professor criticizing a junior student's core thesis will
use maximum indirectness (off-record), while a peer might go bald on-record.

### 1.4 Hedging Devices for Academic Criticism

Hedging is the primary mechanism for softening face-threatening feedback. Categories:

#### Modal Verbs & Adverbs
- "This **may** need reconsideration"
- "The results **might** suggest a different interpretation"
- "**Perhaps** the analysis could benefit from..."
- "This **seems** to indicate..."

#### Probability Qualifiers
- "**Possibly** the sample size is insufficient"
- "**Most likely** there's a confounding variable"
- "**Generally speaking**, this approach tends to..."

#### Introductory Hedging Phrases
- "**It could be argued that**..."
- "**One might suggest that**..."
- "**To our knowledge**..."
- "**We believe**..."

#### Question-Form Softeners
- "**Have you considered** whether...?"
- "**I wonder if** it might be worth..."
- "**Would it be possible** to...?"
- "**Don't you think** that...?"

## 2. Academic Feedback Decoded: What Professors Say vs. What They Mean

### 2.1 The "Say X Mean Y" Pattern

Source: Dr. W. Carter-Veale, University of Maryland faculty panel; various academic blogs

| What Faculty Say | What They Actually Mean | Severity |
|-----------------|------------------------|----------|
| "Would you like to change or fix this?" | "You NEED to change or fix this." (Not a choice) | HIGH |
| "Have you considered X, Y, or Z?" | "You have MISSED X, Y, Z and need to include them." | HIGH |
| "Make those changes when you can." | "Make those changes NOW." | MEDIUM |
| "This is an interesting approach..." | "I have serious reservations about this approach." | HIGH |
| "That's not bad." | "That's actually quite good." (British understatement) | LOW (positive) |
| "Very interesting." | "This is clearly nonsense." (ironic) | CRITICAL |
| "I hear what you say." | "I disagree and do not want to discuss it further." | HIGH |
| "With the greatest respect..." | "I think you are completely wrong." | CRITICAL |
| "That is a very brave proposal." | "You are insane." | CRITICAL |
| "Quite good." | "A bit disappointing." | MEDIUM |
| "Perhaps you could think about..." | "You definitely need to do this." | HIGH |
| "I'm sure it's my fault." | "It's your fault." | MEDIUM |
| "You must come for dinner." | "This is not a real invitation." | LOW |
| "I almost agree." | "I don't agree at all." | HIGH |
| "I only have a few minor comments." | "Please rewrite completely." (reviewer code) | CRITICAL |
| "Could we consider some other options?" | "Your idea is terrible." | HIGH |

### 2.2 British Academic-Speak Translation Guide

Origin: Anglo-Dutch business translation chart, widely adapted for academia.

| British Academic Says | They Mean | Non-British Academic Understands |
|-----------------------|-----------|--------------------------------|
| "I hear what you say" | I disagree and do not want to discuss it | They accept my point of view |
| "With the greatest respect" | I think you are wrong | They are listening to me |
| "That's not bad" | That's good | That's poor |
| "Quite good" | A bit disappointing | Quite good |
| "That is an original point of view" | You must be mad | They think I'm creative |
| "I would suggest..." | Do it or face the consequences | Think about it, but do what you want |
| "Oh, by the way..." | The primary purpose of this discussion is... | This is not important |
| "I was a bit disappointed that..." | I am furious | It doesn't really matter |
| "Very interesting" | I don't agree/I don't believe you | They are impressed |
| "I'll bear it in mind" | I will do nothing about it | They will probably do it |
| "I'm sure it's my fault" | It is your fault | It was their fault |
| "You must come for dinner" | It's not an invitation, I'm being polite | I will get an invitation soon |
| "I almost agree" | I don't agree at all | They almost agree |
| "Perhaps you would consider..." | This is an order | Think about it if you wish |

### 2.3 Reviewer Comment Decoded

| Reviewer Writes | Reviewer Means |
|----------------|----------------|
| "The paper would benefit from additional analysis" | Your analysis is insufficient |
| "The authors may wish to consider..." | You must do this or face rejection |
| "It is not entirely clear..." | I have no idea what you're saying |
| "The novelty of this work is somewhat limited" | This is not novel |
| "Some concerns remain regarding..." | Major flaw that must be fixed |
| "The manuscript could be strengthened by..." | It's currently weak |
| "While the approach is interesting..." | ...it doesn't work |
| "The literature review could be more comprehensive" | You missed key papers (possibly mine) |

## 3. Power Dynamics in Academic Meetings

### 3.1 Structural Power Sources

Source: Science magazine career articles; conversation analysis research

**Adviser power over students includes:**
- Control of financial support (can fire you)
- Control of facility access
- Power of the pen (degree completion)
- Recommendation letters (career gate)
- Publication authorship decisions
- Research direction authority

**Key insight**: "No matter how friendly and egalitarian advisers might try to be, they
wield enormous power over their students." -- Peter Fiske, Science career columnist

### 3.2 Turn-Taking and Speech Rights

From conversation analysis research:

- **Supervisors produce significantly more cognitive verbs than students** (81% vs 19%
  in Swedish academic supervision data, Nelson & Henricson 2019)
- Supervisors' frequent use of cognitive verbs ("I think," "I believe," "I wonder")
  relates to their institutional task of **giving feedback and modifying its directness**
- **Equivocation in feedback serves a pedagogical purpose**: balancing the institutional
  goal of teaching with the expectation that PhD students should be competent researchers

### 3.3 Turn-Taking Hierarchy in Academic Meetings

| Rank | Who | Turn-Taking Behavior |
|------|-----|---------------------|
| 1 | Senior PI / Department Head | Speaks first and last; sets agenda; can interrupt anyone |
| 2 | Co-advisors / Associate Professors | Speak after PI; can redirect discussion |
| 3 | Senior PhD students / Postdocs | Can volunteer comments; may challenge peers |
| 4 | Junior PhD students | Typically speak only when called upon or during Q&A |
| 5 | Master's students / Presenter | Respond to questions; lowest speech initiative rights |

### 3.4 Common Power Plays in Academic Settings

| Power Play | How It Works | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Agenda control** | Setting what gets discussed, in what order | PI always reviews their priorities first |
| **Time allocation** | Giving more/less time signals importance | Cutting short a student's presentation |
| **Question as command** | Framing orders as questions | "Have you considered doing X?" = "Do X" |
| **Strategic silence** | Not commenting = silent disapproval | PI says nothing after your presentation |
| **Praise withdrawal** | Absence of usual encouragement | Normally says "good work" but stays silent |
| **Public vs. private feedback** | Public praise, private criticism (or reverse) | Waiting until after meeting to deliver harsh feedback |
| **Storytelling as instruction** | Telling anecdotes that carry implicit lessons | "I once had a student who..." |
| **Delegated criticism** | Having a senior student deliver the PI's critique | "Why don't you tell them about the issue with..." |

## 4. Q&A Survival: Defending Research Under Pressure

### 4.1 Taxonomy of Question Types (Kshitij Tiwari Framework)

| Category | Intent | Response Strategy |
|----------|--------|-------------------|
| **Methodology Defense** | Why did you choose this approach? | Justify with trade-off analysis, not excuses |
| **Novelty Police** | How is this different from existing work? | Pivot from similarities to your unique delta |
| **Data Doubters** | Are your results reliable? | Reinforce process integrity |
| **Scope & Limitations** | Isn't this outside your study? | Gracefully decline without appearing to have missed it |
| **The Unknown** | Question you can't answer | "Professional Pivot" -- admit gap while maintaining authority |
| **Hostile Reviewer** | Aggressive/rude questioning | De-escalate; redirect to substance |
| **Clarification** | Can you explain this simply? | Simplify without dumbing down |
| **Future & Impact** | "So what?" | Connect to broader significance |
| **Hypotheticals** | "What if X were different?" | Acknowledge without speculating wildly |
| **Reflective** | What did you learn? | Show intellectual growth |

### 4.2 Response Templates (Christopher Watkin's 15 Tips)

**Template 1: Restate and Redirect**
> "You are asking, I think, about [X]. I'd like to pick up on your point that [Y]..."
> -- Allows you to choose which part of a multi-part question to answer

**Template 2: Clarification Request**
> "Just let me make sure I've understood. Your question is about ____, right?"
> -- Buys time and ensures accurate response

**Template 3: The Cricket Bat Defense**
> Know your main thesis. Evaluate if the question attacks:
> a) Your main thesis (critical -- must address)
> b) A supporting strand (important -- address then note thesis still holds)
> c) Nothing you actually said (deflect politely)

**Template 4: Concession Without Surrender**
> "Even if I grant you that point, the main thesis of my paper still holds because..."

**Template 5: The Honest Unknown**
> "That's an excellent question I haven't fully explored yet. Based on what I do know,
> [partial answer], and I'd welcome the opportunity to investigate this further."

### 4.3 Key Defense Principles

1. **Prepare for Q&A as seriously as for the presentation** -- "People often remember
   the exchanges after a paper more than the paper itself"
2. **Assume good faith** -- Interpret questions as friendly until proven otherwise
3. **Keep answers to 30-60 seconds** -- Don't turn answers into mini-papers
4. **Use pen and paper** -- Note key words during questions to avoid forgetting
5. **The 2-second rule** -- Pause before answering; shows thoughtfulness, not panic

## 5. Conflict Triggers and Resolution in Academic Settings

### 5.1 Common Conflict Triggers

Source: Science magazine; research group management literature

| Trigger | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Authorship disputes** | Order of names, credit allocation, ghost authorship |
| **Research direction disagreements** | Student wants X, advisor wants Y |
| **Workload expectations** | "You should be working weekends" vs. work-life balance |
| **Financial support changes** | Grants running out, funding withdrawn |
| **Methodology disagreements** | Which approach to use, experimental design |
| **Publication timing** | When to publish, whether results are ready |
| **Graduation timeline** | Student ready to defend, advisor wants more work |
| **Intellectual ownership** | Whose idea was it? Who gets credit? |
| **Personality clashes** | Communication style mismatch |
| **Competence questions** | Advisor doubts student ability, or vice versa |

### 5.2 Resolution Strategies (Principled Negotiation for Academia)

Source: Peter Fiske, Science career articles; Fisher & Ury "Getting to Yes"

1. **Separate people from problems**: Distinguish personality clashes from substantive
   disagreements. "Understanding the specific issues--the number of experiments, the
   size of your thesis--and separating them from the mismatch in personal styles"

2. **Focus on interests, not positions**: Behind "You must submit 4 papers" might be
   "I need the lab's publication record strong." Behind "Let me graduate in June" might
   be "I have a job offer with a deadline."

3. **Invent options for mutual gain**: Create creative alternatives rather than either/or

4. **Insist on objective criteria**: "Is 4 papers the standard for all students in the
   department? What does the committee require?"

### 5.3 Warning Signs of Relationship Deterioration

| Signal | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| Advisor declines to chair your committee | May be losing confidence in you |
| "Grant money has run out, can't fund you" | Possibly losing confidence in your ability |
| Research stalled, no meetings scheduled | Advisor may have disengaged |
| Advisor redirects you to other faculty | May be trying to transfer you out |
| Feedback becomes minimal or absent | Worse than harsh feedback -- signals abandonment |

## 6. Western vs. Chinese Academic Culture: Comparison Points

### 6.1 Communication Style

| Dimension | Western Academic Culture | Chinese Academic Culture |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------------|
| **Directness** | Values explicit, direct communication; indirectness may seem "devious" | Values indirect communication; relies on context, tone, nonverbal cues |
| **"No" expression** | Can say "I disagree" directly | Uses "perhaps," silence, subject change, or "this needs further study" |
| **Feedback delivery** | Often direct but hedged; "sandwich" feedback (praise-criticism-praise) | Heavily indirect among peers; can be brutally direct from senior to junior |
| **Questioning authority** | Encouraged as "critical thinking" | Risky; may be seen as "challenging authority" (挑战权威) |

### 6.2 Hierarchy and Power

| Dimension | Western Academic Culture | Chinese Academic Culture |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------------|
| **Advisor title** | "Advisor" or "supervisor" | "老板" (boss) -- reveals the true power dynamic |
| **Hierarchy rigidity** | Relatively flat; junior can debate senior | Strict hierarchy; respect follows seniority |
| **Challenging superiors** | Acceptable with evidence and diplomacy | First major taboo in Chinese labs |
| **Decision making** | Collaborative discussion expected | Top-down; advisor's word is final |
| **Student autonomy** | Expected to develop independence | Expected to execute advisor's vision |
| **Group identity** | Individual researcher identity | "师门" (mentor-family) collective identity |

### 6.3 Face Management

| Dimension | Western Academic Culture | Chinese Academic Culture |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------------|
| **Face concept** | Important but secondary to "truth" | Central organizing principle (面子/脸) |
| **Public criticism** | Acceptable if constructive | Taboo; causes severe "丢脸" (loss of face) |
| **Admitting error** | Can say "I was wrong" directly | Must be done indirectly; "I need to check my data" |
| **Giving face** | Praise for good work | Strategic social currency (给面子); creates obligation |
| **Intermediaries** | Less common; direct resolution preferred | Common for sensitive conflicts (中间人) |
| **Saving face for others** | Nice but optional | Social obligation; shared responsibility |

### 6.4 Meeting Dynamics

| Dimension | Western Academic Culture | Chinese Academic Culture |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------------|
| **Who speaks first** | Often the presenter, then open floor | Senior professor sets the tone first |
| **Question style** | Direct challenges welcome | Questions wrapped in "请教" (seeking guidance) framing |
| **Silence meaning** | Awkward; someone should speak | Can mean deep consideration OR strong disagreement |
| **Conflict expression** | Open debate in meeting | Private discussion after meeting |
| **Praise style** | Specific and earned | May be strategic face-giving; decode carefully |

## 7. Actionable Dialogue Template Patterns

### 7.1 Universal Criticism Softening Formulas

**Formula 1: Hedge + Observation + Question**
> "I might be wrong, but it seems like [observation]. Have you considered [alternative]?"

**Formula 2: Praise + Pivot + Suggestion**
> "The [positive aspect] is really strong. Building on that, what if we also [suggestion]?"

**Formula 3: Self-deprecation + Challenge**
> "I'm probably missing something, but how does [X] account for [problem]?"

**Formula 4: Hypothetical Reframe**
> "If a reviewer were to raise [concern], how would you respond?"

**Formula 5: Third-Party Attribution**
> "I've seen some papers argue that [counter-position]. How do you see your work in relation to that?"

### 7.2 Power-Stratified Response Templates

**Responding to someone MORE senior:**
> "That's a really valuable perspective. Could I ask about [specific aspect] to make sure
> I understand the implications for [your work]?"

**Responding to a PEER:**
> "I see your point, but I'd push back slightly on [specific claim] because [evidence]."

**Responding to someone MORE junior:**
> "That's a great question, and it gets at something important. The reason is [explanation],
> which connects to [broader context]."

### 7.3 Deflection and Buying Time

**"I need more time" variants:**
> - "That's worth thinking about more carefully than I can in the moment."
> - "Let me look into that and follow up with you after the meeting."
> - "That touches on something I'm still working through in the analysis."

**"I don't know" variants:**
> - "I haven't explored that specific angle yet, but my intuition based on [X] is..."
> - "That's outside the scope of this study, but it would make excellent future work."
> - "I'd want to check the literature on that before giving a definitive answer."

### 7.4 Escalation/De-escalation Patterns

**De-escalation (when discussion gets heated):**
> - "I think we're actually closer to agreement than it seems. We both want [shared goal]."
> - "Let me make sure I understand your concern correctly before responding."
> - "You raise a fair point. Let me think about how that interacts with [my position]."

**Controlled escalation (when you need to push back):**
> - "I appreciate the suggestion, but I have to respectfully disagree because [evidence]."
> - "The data actually tells a different story here. If we look at [specific evidence]..."
> - "I understand the concern, but that interpretation doesn't account for [key factor]."
